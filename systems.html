Systems Model

Understanding the interconnected factors affecting student progression through a systems-thinking lens

Systems-Thinking Approach

Rather than viewing student progression challenges as isolated problems, we employ systems-thinking to understand the complex interdependencies between multiple factors. This holistic approach reveals leverage points for effective intervention.

Core Principles

  • Interconnectedness: Student progression is influenced by multiple interconnected variables
  • Feedback Loops: Outcomes feed back to influence future inputs and behaviours
  • Leverage Points: Small changes in strategic areas can yield significant system-wide improvements
  • Emergence: System behaviour is not simply the sum of individual parts

Key System Components

1. Student Level Factors

Demographics

Age, socioeconomic background, first-generation status, language background

Academic Background

Prior educational attainment, academic preparedness, subject knowledge

Personal Attributes

Motivation, engagement, self-efficacy, resilience, learning strategies

Life Circumstances

Work commitments, family responsibilities, health, financial situation

2. Programme Level Factors

Curriculum Design

Relevance, accessibility, alignment with learning outcomes, progression

Teaching & Learning

Pedagogical approaches, assessment methods, feedback quality

Support Services

Academic support, personal wellbeing, career guidance, technology access

Programme Culture

Sense of belonging, peer support, staff-student relationships

3. Institutional Level Factors

Governance & Policy

Institutional values, progression policies, resource allocation

Infrastructure

Physical facilities, digital infrastructure, support systems

Coordination

Cross-departmental communication, data systems, evidence use

External Context

Labour market demands, societal expectations, competitive landscape

Key Feedback Loops

The system contains several important feedback mechanisms:

Reinforcing Loops (Positive)

  • Success Momentum: Early academic success builds confidence → increases engagement → further success
  • Community Building: Positive peer interactions → sense of belonging → deeper engagement
  • Support Effectiveness: Timely intervention → improved outcomes → increased service utilization

Balancing Loops (Challenges)

  • Resource Constraints: Limited support capacity → longer waiting times → reduced effectiveness
  • Disengagement Spiral: Early difficulties → reduced engagement → further difficulties
  • Silos: Lack of coordination → fragmented support → gaps in provision

System Dynamics Over Time

Student progression is not static but evolves through phases:

Critical Transition Points

  • Entry: First semester challenges, transition support, initial engagement
  • Early Programme: Building academic skills, establishing peer networks, clarifying goals
  • Mid-Programme: Consolidation, deepening learning, managing workload
  • Completion: Capstone experiences, career preparation, transition support

Targeted interventions at these critical points can have magnified impact due to system sensitivity.
