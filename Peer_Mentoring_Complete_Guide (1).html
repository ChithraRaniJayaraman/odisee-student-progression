Section 01

What Is Peer Mentoring?

Peer mentoring is one of the most human things a university can offer. It is when a student who has already navigated the hard parts of first year sits with a student who is going through those same hard parts right now — and shows them the way through.

A real, honest relationship between two people — one who knows the terrain, one who is learning it. Helping a student find the right desk, ask the right question, know they are not alone — not as a service, but as a person.

A mentor who studied in a second language, who was the first in their family at university, who juggled money and deadlines and self-doubt — that mentor is worth more to a matching mentee than any formal support service. Because they already proved it is possible.

Understanding the Evidence — EEF Mentoring vs Higher Education Research

The EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit (2025) rates mentoring at +2 months of average additional progress. This is distinct from peer tutoring (+5 months), which involves structured academic content delivery. Our programme is peer mentoring — focused on belonging, guidance, and support, not course content. The EEF evidence base is drawn primarily from school-age (K-12) populations. In higher education — specifically for first-generation and migrant-background students — the evidence is significantly stronger: Torres et al. (2025) demonstrated sustained gains in belonging and academic performance in a randomised controlled trial; García et al. (2024) found significantly lower dropout rates across 3,774 university students; and Gehreke et al. (2024) confirmed that structured mentoring with trained mentors produces measurably better outcomes than informal peer support across all higher education studies reviewed.

Core Definition

Peer mentoring pairs first-year students with trained senior students who have successfully navigated the same challenges. At Odisee Brussels, this means pairing students from migrant and low-income backgrounds with mentors who understand — from lived experience — what it feels like to arrive at university without a map. This is not tutoring. Mentors do not teach course content. They offer something more valuable: connection, practical guidance, and the reassurance that what the mentee is experiencing is normal — and navigable.

Section 02

Why Odisee Brussels — Why Now?

The evidence for a peer mentoring programme at Odisee Brussels is not theoretical. It comes from our own institutional data, confirmed by our partner, and validated by 40+ sources in the academic literature.

Elien Crois, researcher at Odisee's Service for Innovation, Education and Quality Assurance, was direct in our meeting: students at the Brussels campus — especially those with a migrant background — show significantly lower study efficiency than students at any other Odisee campus. The numbers point to a structural problem, not an individual one.

Many of these students arrive without the invisible preparation other students take for granted. They may not know how to ask a lecturer for help, or how to catch themselves before falling behind. When financial stress, language barriers, and a feeling of not belonging pile on together, students do not suddenly decide to quit. They slowly drift away — and often before anyone has noticed.

The Core Challenge — What the Research Confirms

The biggest predictors of first-year struggle at Odisee Brussels are: lack of structured study habits (Van Rooij et al., 2018); absence of belonging and peer connection (Gopalan & Brady, 2020); financial stress impairing working memory (Almarzouki, 2024); and first-generation student status (Barsegyan & Maas, 2024). None of these are fixed characteristics. All of them can be addressed through targeted, proactive support.

Section 04

Every Session — The Five-Step Flow

Every bi-weekly meeting follows the same five-step structure. This is not a rigid script — it is a reliable shape. Structure gives mentors confidence and gives mentees the safety of knowing what to expect. Sessions are one hour. Bi-weekly. On campus or online.

110 min

Check In — How Are You Really?

Trust first

Start with one genuine question about how the person's week went — not about grades, not about assignments. This step builds the trust that makes everything else in the session possible. It signals: I am here for you as a person, not as a student file.

215 min

The Plate — What's Coming Up?

Make it manageable

The mentee shares what is coming up — deadlines, assignments, group work, exams. Together, you turn a wall into a list. Breaking things down is often all it takes to make them feel manageable. You do not do it for them.

320 min

The Real Conversation — Heart of Every Session

Experience matters

Share what actually helped you — not perfect answers, but honest ones. "When I had that module, I found that..." is worth more than any advice sheet. The mentee gets to ask questions they would never ask a lecturer. This is where trust becomes growth.

410 min

One Small Next Step

Specific action

Together agree on one specific action before the next meeting. Not "study harder." Something like: "Go to the student support desk on Tuesday" or "Send that email today." Small and specific means it actually happens.

55 min

Close and Confirm — Every Single Time

Commitment

Confirm the next meeting before you leave. A confirmed next meeting is a commitment — for both of you. It signals: this is a relationship, not a one-off conversation.

Section 05

Mentor Training — Before Any Session Happens

No mentor simply shows up and figures it out. Every mentor completes a structured one-day training before their first session. The EEF is clear: even the most evidence-backed intervention fails without careful implementation. Training matters. Structure matters. Feedback loops matter.

Why Training Is Non-Negotiable

Gehreke et al. (2024), in a systematic review of peer mentoring programmes, found that the single biggest difference between programmes that reduced dropout and programmes that did not was structure. Structured programmes with trained mentors significantly outperformed informal peer support. Training is what turns goodwill into impact.

Section 06

SWOT Analysis

An honest look at where this programme is strong, where it needs careful management, what opportunities it can build on, and what threats the implementation plan already addresses.

Strengths

  • Peer-to-peer trust — more accessible than any formal staff support
  • Lived experience addresses cultural and language barriers directly
  • Connects all three tools as the primary student relationship
  • Low cost — uses existing student community, no new budget
  • Mentors gain professional development and a formal certificate

Weaknesses

  • Quality depends on consistent coordinator oversight
  • Mentor wellbeing must be actively monitored and protected
  • Matching process requires care and cultural sensitivity
  • Some students may initially resist or distrust the relationship

Opportunities

  • Directly aligns with Odisee's inclusion goals and policy (2022–2026)
  • Scalable across all Brussels campus programmes
  • Builds a culture of proactive care across the institution
  • Pilot data from Year 1 builds the case for permanent embedding

Threats

  • Mentor dropout if wellbeing is not actively managed
  • Stigma risk — students may feel labelled if not handled with care
  • Without coordinator follow-through, outreach becomes a gesture
  • Informal drift — sessions become social chats without structure

Section 09

Mentor Wellbeing — You Matter Too

Mentors are students too. They carry their own academic workload, financial pressures, and personal lives — and then they take on the emotional weight of someone else's struggles. Without care for mentors, the best programmes burn out their best people.

Every mentor has a head mentor who checks in every five weeks — not to inspect, but to listen. "How are you finding it?" is a question mentors deserve to be asked too. Mentors are told from Day 1: if something a mentee shares stays with you after the session, that is a signal to talk to your head mentor. Not a failure — a signal.

The Mentor Wellbeing Protocol — Three Rules

Rule 1: No mentor carries a Red-level concern alone. Ever. They flag it immediately. The coordinator manages it from there.

Rule 2: Head mentors check in on every mentor every five weeks — not just the ones who seem to be struggling.

Rule 3: The coordinator maintains a simple record of mentor check-ins. If a mentor misses two consecutive check-ins without explanation, the coordinator reaches out directly within 48 hours.

When It Does Not Work — The Mismatch Protocol

Not every pairing works. Sometimes the chemistry is simply not there. This is not a failure — it is a reality that the programme anticipates and manages. A student who stays in a mismatched pairing loses the benefit of the programme entirely. A student who is rematched has a second chance at the relationship that makes the difference.

Mismatch Protocol — Three Steps

Step 1: Either mentor or mentee raises concern with head mentor. This is explicitly framed as a normal part of the programme — not a complaint.

Step 2: Head mentor has a confidential conversation with both parties separately. If both confirm the pairing is not working, the coordinator is notified within 48 hours.

Step 3: Coordinator facilitates rematch within one week. Both parties receive a brief check-in session before the new pairing begins.

Real World Universities — 8 International Examples

Eight independently evaluated peer mentoring programmes from across the world — what they did, how they did it, what happened, and what Odisee can take from each one. Every source links to the original published study or university page.

How to Read This Section

These are not papers written by your own research group. They are independently run, externally evaluated programmes at real universities. Each card shows: what the programme actually did, the key metrics, the results, and — most importantly — what Odisee can take directly from each case. Every "What Odisee Can Take" note is based on what the researchers themselves found worked or didn't.

🇪🇸
Spanish University Peer Mentoring — García et al. (2024)
Spain · 3,774 students · Quasi-experimental design · Published in Revista de Psicología

What Actually Happened
A Spanish university compared 1,887 students who participated in a peer mentoring programme against 1,887 matched students who did not — same courses, same intake year, same background profile. Mentors were trained senior students. Mentees attended structured sessions throughout the year. The researchers measured two things: dropout rates and academic performance. Mentees had significantly lower dropout rates than the control group — and this held across every subject area studied. Academic performance was also measurably higher. Critically, the effect existed regardless of discipline — Social Work, Business, Engineering — all showed the same pattern. The relationship is what matters, not the subject.

✓ Significantly lower dropout for mentees✓ Higher academic performance across all subjects✓ Effect consistent regardless of discipline
What Odisee Can Take From This

This is the study to cite when any Odisee stakeholder asks "does peer mentoring actually reduce dropout?" The answer from 3,774 real students in a Spanish university setting is yes — significantly, consistently, and across all subject areas. The context is very close to Odisee Brussels: public university, mixed student backgrounds, structured programme.

🇪🇸
Spanish University — 5 Years Including Pandemic (Alonso-García et al., 2025)
Spain · 4,962 students · 5 academic years 2018–2023 · Including COVID-19

What Actually Happened
This is one of the most powerful studies in peer mentoring research — because it tracked 4,962 students over five full academic years, including the COVID-19 pandemic. When campuses closed and every support system was disrupted, peer mentoring continued — and continued to reduce dropout. The programme worked even through the pandemic. This is evidence that peer mentoring is not a fair-weather intervention. It is resilient precisely because it is a human relationship, not an institutional service.

✓ Dropout reduction held through pandemic✓ Programme resilient across disruption✓ Strongest evidence for long-term effect
What Odisee Can Take From This

When someone at Odisee asks "will this still work when things are disrupted?" — this is the answer. Peer mentoring kept reducing dropout even when the institution around it was in crisis. Build the programme to be resilient from the start: train mentors to hold sessions online if needed, confirm the next meeting before leaving every session.

Alonso-García et al. (2025) — ScienceDirect →

🇳🇱
Utrecht University — Bias-Trained Peer Mentors (Van der Velden et al., 2023)
Netherlands · School of Medicine · 25% migrant background · Two-year comparison study

What Actually Happened
Utrecht has a student population where 25% have a migrant background — very similar to Brussels. The School of Medicine assigned every first-year student a peer mentor from Day 1. The critical design choice was mentor training: Utrecht added explicit bias-awareness and cultural competence training to the standard mentor programme. The comparison group had mentors who were not bias-trained. Students mentored by bias-trained mentors reported significantly higher sense of belonging and lower anxiety about seeking help. The difference was not the mentoring — it was the cultural competence of the mentor.

✓ Higher belonging for mentees of bias-trained mentors✓ Lower anxiety about seeking help✓ Cultural competence was the decisive variable
What Odisee Can Take From This

Add a half-day "cultural awareness in mentoring" block to the Odisee mentor training day. Brussels students come from 50+ nationalities. A mentor who knows that a student going quiet may not be disengaged — they may be from a culture where speaking up in a one-to-one feels inappropriate — will run completely different sessions. This single addition is what the Utrecht data shows makes the biggest difference for diverse campuses.

Van der Velden et al. (2023) — BMC Medical Education →

🇬🇧
University of Manchester — AMBS Peer Mentoring Scheme
Manchester, UK · Alliance Manchester Business School · All first-year students · Running continuously

What Actually Happened
Manchester runs one of the UK's best-documented peer mentoring schemes. Every single first-year student is assigned to a pair of peer mentors before Welcome Week — not after the year has started, before. The scheme is embedded in the programme structure, not offered as an optional extra. The key design choice: Manchester explicitly frames the mentor as "someone to ask things you wouldn't ask a lecturer" — normalising the questions that first-generation and international students are most afraid to ask. The programme has run continuously for over a decade. That longevity is the result: it works, so it stays.

✓ Universal coverage — no student left unassigned✓ Programme embedded, not optional✓ 10+ years of continuous operation
What Odisee Can Take From This

Manchester's framing — "ask what you can't ask a lecturer" — should go on the Odisee mentor card. It removes the shame from the most basic questions: Where do I print? How do I email a professor? What happens if I miss a deadline? These are the questions that first-generation students are most afraid to ask — and a peer mentor is the right person to answer them.

🌍
Refugee & Migrant Mentoring — Action Research Study (PMC, 2024)
International · Refugee and migrant students · 12-week programme · Qualitative evaluation

What Actually Happened
This action research study specifically examined mentoring programmes for refugee and migrant students — the closest published equivalent to Odisee Brussels's student profile. Over a 12-week programme, mentors and mentees worked on practical integration challenges: language, documentation, accessing services, understanding institutional expectations. Both mentors and mentees reported significant gains. Mentees reported improved study habits, increased sense of purpose, and reduced isolation. Mentors reported gains in empathy, cultural humility, and leadership confidence. The study validated a key insight: the mentoring relationship benefits both directions simultaneously.

✓ Mentees: better study habits, less isolation✓ Mentors: leadership and empathy gains✓ Benefits flow in both directions
What Odisee Can Take From This

This study validates two things in the Odisee toolkit simultaneously: the mentee gains (study habits, motivation, sense of purpose) and the mentor gains (leadership, empathy, cultural competence). When pitching the programme to second-year students, lead with what they gain — not just what they give.

Migrant mentoring action research (ScienceDirect, 2024) →

🇩🇪
University of Augsburg — Digital Peer Mentoring (Drossard & Härtl, 2024)
Germany · First-year medical students · Started during COVID · Moved to in-person · Made compulsory

What Actually Happened
A newly founded medical school in Germany launched peer mentoring during COVID — entirely online — because it had no choice. Seventeen trained peer mentors were each assigned to a small group of 6–7 first-year students. Overall satisfaction was high for both mentors and mentees. After COVID, the programme moved to in-person delivery and was formally embedded in the core curriculum — meaning it became compulsory, not optional. The lesson: a programme that works online can become stronger in person, and one that works in crisis can become an institution's permanent foundation.

✓ High satisfaction for mentors and mentees✓ Online to in-person transition successful✓ Programme made compulsory after pilot
What Odisee Can Take From This

Run the Odisee programme as a pilot in Year 1, evaluate it honestly, and build the case for making it a permanent embedded part of the programme — not a voluntary extra. The Augsburg case shows this transition is not only possible — it strengthens the programme. A pilot that works becomes an institution.

Drossard & Härtl (2024) — GMS Journal of Medical Education →

🇦🇺
University of Melbourne — Faculty of Medicine, Dentistry & Health Sciences
Melbourne, Australia · Melbourne Plus digital credential · Diverse cohort · Ongoing programme

What Actually Happened
Melbourne runs peer mentoring across the Faculty of Medicine, Dentistry and Health Sciences, with a specific focus on cultural diversity. Mentors receive specialised professional development and are awarded a verified digital credential — the Melbourne Plus badge — for completing the programme. This credential is recognised by employers and listed on mentors' official university record. The digital badge significantly increased mentor recruitment and retention — because it made the value of participating visible and portable. Mentors no longer had to explain what they had done; the badge did it for them.

✓ Digital badge increased mentor recruitment✓ Employer-recognised credential✓ Mentor retention significantly improved
What Odisee Can Take From This

Instead of — or in addition to — a paper certificate, give Odisee mentors a verifiable digital badge (via Credly or a similar platform). A credential that mentors can share on LinkedIn and attach to a CV makes participation a professional development asset, not just a volunteer activity. It costs little to implement and significantly increases the quality of mentor applications.

Gehreke et al. (2024) — Systematic Review of All Peer Mentoring Research
Global · Review of Education journal · 17+ studies · What works across ALL programmes

What Actually Happened
Gehreke and colleagues reviewed all available peer mentoring research in higher education and synthesised what works, what doesn't, and what makes programmes fail. Their finding on structure is the most important for Odisee: structured programmes with trained mentors significantly outperformed informal peer support in every outcome measured. Programmes that gave mentors no training, no session format, and no check-in system consistently produced weaker results — or no results at all. The other key finding: programmes that monitored mentor wellbeing had significantly lower mentor dropout rates — and better mentee outcomes as a consequence.

✓ Structured > informal in every outcome✓ Training is the decisive factor✓ Mentor wellbeing monitoring = better outcomes
What Odisee Can Take From This

This is your answer to anyone who says "can't mentors just chat informally?" No. Structured sessions with a clear five-step format produce measurably better outcomes than informal peer support in every single study Gehreke reviewed. The Odisee toolkit's five-step session structure and one-day training are not bureaucracy — they are what makes the programme work.

What All Eight Programmes Have in Common

Across eight countries, thousands of students, and more than a decade of implementation, the evidence converges on the same conclusions. These are the non-negotiable principles that every successful peer mentoring programme shares.

🏗️
Principle 1
Structure is what makes it work

Informal "chat with a senior student" produces no measurable effect. A five-step session structure, a check-in note, and a coordinator review system produce significant results.

🎓
Principle 2
Training is non-negotiable

Every programme that worked trained its mentors before the first session. Every programme that failed or underperformed did not. Training is not overhead — it is the intervention.

🌍
Principle 3
Cultural competence is the variable

Utrecht showed it clearly: bias-trained mentors produced significantly better outcomes for migrant-background students. Cultural awareness training is not optional at Odisee Brussels.

❤️
Principle 4
Mentor wellbeing drives mentee outcomes

Programmes that monitored and supported mentor wellbeing had lower mentor dropout — and measurably better mentee outcomes. You cannot give what you do not have.

🏛️
Principle 5
Pilots become permanent when they work

Augsburg, Melbourne, Manchester — all started as pilots and became embedded institutional programmes. The path from "let's try this" to "this is how we do things here" is one honest evaluation.

↔️
Principle 6
Benefits are mutual

Mentors gain leadership, cultural competence, and professional credentials. When you recruit mentors, lead with what they gain — not just what they give. Both are real, both matter.
